\section{Dense feed-forward block as the replacement target}
\label{sec:ch02-dense-feed-forward-block-as-the-replacement-target}

Section~\ref{sec:ch02-causal-self-attention-as-the-context-path} mixed positions across the sequence; what is left in a decoder layer is the per-token transformation that runs after attention. That block is the dense FFN of Section~\ref{sec:ch01-the-dense-ffn-bottleneck} --- the same class, the same constructor signature \texttt{DenseFFN(d\_model, hidden)}, the same $2 D H$ parameter count. Chapter~1 already counted what it costs; this section installs it inside the chapter-2 baseline and frames it as the \emph{replacement target}. Every MoE variant the rest of the book builds is a drop-in substitute for this object.

\input{figures/ch02/fig-dense-feed-forward-block-as-the-replacement-target}

Figure~\ref{fig:ch02-dense-feed-forward-block-as-the-replacement-target} draws the skeleton that survives unchanged into Chapter~3. Today (top row) a single \texttt{DenseFFN} sits between a \texttt{(B, T, D) $\to$ (B$\cdot$T, D)} flatten and the inverse unflatten; tomorrow (bottom row) the same skeleton wraps a bank of expert FFNs and a router picks $K$ of them per token. The $(B, T, D)$ tensor on either end of the skeleton does not move, which is the entire reason the FFN sub-layer can be swapped without touching attention, embeddings, or the training loop.

Take the chapter-2 anchor mini-example: $B = 2$, $T = 8$, $D = 16$. Pick $H = 4 D = 64$, the standard expansion factor used throughout the book. The block stores $2 D H = 2048$ parameters and spends $2 D H = 2048$ multiply-adds per token, which is the cost we counted in Section~\ref{sec:ch01-the-dense-ffn-bottleneck} --- nothing has changed except where the module lives. For the full $B \cdot T = 16$ tokens of the anchor batch the dense FFN charges $2 N D H = 32{,}768$ multiply-adds.

Name the new symbols. We keep $B, T, D, H, N$ from Section~\ref{sec:ch01-the-dense-ffn-bottleneck}. The only new objects here are the two helper functions: \texttt{flatten\_tokens(x)} returns the $(B \cdot T, D)$ view of $x$ along with the $(B, T)$ tuple needed to restore the shape, and \texttt{unflatten\_tokens(flat, (B, T))} is its inverse. We will use this pair literally unchanged when Chapter~6 vectorises dispatch.

Equation~\ref{eq:ch02-dense-feed-forward-block-as-the-replacement-target} reads carefully: the subscript $(b, t)$ on the left and the right makes the per-token structure explicit. The right-hand side never indexes another row of the batch and never indexes another position in the sequence. Cross-token mixing is the job of attention; the FFN sub-layer is purely a token-wise non-linearity. That is the property the MoE replacement preserves --- the per-token \emph{transformation} can change from token to token, but the transformation is still per-token.

The example script under \texttt{examples/ch02/} threads the seed-$0$ anchor batch through embed $\to$ attention $\to$ dense FFN, prints the parameter count, and verifies two things at once: the output shape is exactly $(B, T, D)$, and the FFN output is byte-identical whether the input is given as a $(B, T, D)$ tensor or as a flattened $(B \cdot T, D)$ tensor followed by an unflatten. That equality is the strongest possible test of ``per-token'' --- any cross-token mixing inside the block, intentional or accidental, breaks it.
